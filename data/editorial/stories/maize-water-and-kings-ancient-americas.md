"Ancient Americas" is a vast label. It stretches across two continents and more than two thousand years, and it covers peoples who never met, spoke unrelated languages and built very different societies: the Chavín of the Peruvian Andes, the city dwellers of Teotihuacan, the Maya kingdoms of the lowlands, the Moche and Chimú of the Peruvian coast, the Mexica (Aztec) of Tenochtitlan, and many more. It would be a mistake to flatten them into one story.

And yet, looking across the works from these cultures in the collection, certain concerns recur with remarkable consistency. Water and its absence. The growth of maize. The ruler's responsibility for both. And the conviction that death and renewal are linked in a single, turning cycle. This essay follows those threads.

## Rain and the ruler

In agricultural societies, rain is not background weather; it is life or death. Across the Americas, rulers claimed authority partly by presenting themselves as mediators with the forces that brought it.

At Teotihuacan, in the Valley of Mexico, one of the largest cities in the world during the first millennium CE, palace walls were painted with frescoes. A **Mural Fragment Depicting a Maguey Bloodletting Ritual** (500–600 CE) comes from an aristocratic apartment compound there. It shows a rain priest whose speech scroll, the curling sign for voice, is decorated with shells and plants. The prayer, in other words, is made visible, and its content is water and fertility.

{{artwork:14968|inset}}

Far to the south, in Panama, Coclé goldsmiths cast pendants in the form of frogs. The frog's call signals the coming of the rains, and such creatures were seen as summoners of water. Worn by an elite person, a gold frog linked its owner to the obligation of keeping the agricultural cycle turning. In the American Southwest, Ancestral Puebloan potters painted jars with horned serpents and stepped motifs evoking mountains, springs and thunderclouds: a sacred landscape of water, rendered in black on white.

## Maize and rebirth

If rain was the precondition, maize was the reward, and in Mesoamerica it was far more than a crop. Maya creation stories told of the maize god who died and was reborn, and Maya kings identified themselves with him.

That idea is the subject of a painted **Vessel with Dancing Maize Gods** (780–810) from the vicinity of Naranjo, Guatemala. It shows a ruler dressed as the maize god, and the scene accompanies the soul of the deceased into the realm of the dead, from which it would be reborn like a sprouting ear of corn. The vessel was made by an artist named Aj Maxam. We know this because of a second vessel in the collection.

{{artwork:126858|inset}}

The **Water-Lily Vessel**, also by Aj Maxam, carries an elegant pattern of water lilies, emblems of the watery surface of the underworld and of the earth's regenerative power. Below it runs a hieroglyphic text that was the first on a Classic Maya vessel to be deciphered. It gives the artist's name and states that he belonged to the royal lineage of Naranjo. It is a rare and moving thing: a signature from the eighth century, identifying a painter as both artist and noble.

Seven centuries later, the Mexica of Tenochtitlan honoured **Xilonen**, goddess of young maize. A basalt **Head of Xilonen** (1400–1500) shows her with two large ears of maize rising from her headband. A July festival celebrated the first fruits of the season in her name. A ceramic **Ritual Impersonator of the Deity Xipe Totec** shows another side of the same cycle: Xipe Totec, "the Flayed One", presided over the turn from dry season to rains, and in his ceremonies the connection between sacrifice and renewal was made explicit and physical.

> Across very different cultures, the same conviction recurs: life is sustained by a cycle in which death and renewal are bound together.

## Faces of authority

Rulers also needed to be seen. In the Andes, Moche ceramists on the north coast of Peru created **portrait vessels**, the only works from the ancient Andes that can be called portraits in the Euro-American sense. The example in the collection (600–700) captures a face so individual in its features that it seems to record a particular person. Vessels like this were placed in burials, and they may also have travelled as emblems of authority to other elite households.

{{artwork:91620|inset}}

Gold carried a different kind of authority. A **Gold Pectoral with Zoomorphic Face** from the Chavín culture (about 500 BCE) blends human and animal features, expressing the connection between priestly rulers and the forces of nature. More than fifteen centuries later, a Chimú **Ceremonial Knife (Tumi)** of gold inlaid with turquoise shows a figure thought to be Naymlap, legendary founder of the Lambayeque Valley. It was a precious version of a sacrificial tool, carried by a ruler in state ceremonies.

## The stone of Moctezuma

The collection's most politically charged monument is the **Coronation Stone of Moctezuma Xocoyotzin** (about 1503), a carved basalt block marking the beginning of the reign of the Mexica ruler also known as Motecuhzoma II. It originally stood in the sacred precinct of Tenochtitlan, whose ruins lie beneath Mexico City.

{{artwork:75644|wide|Coronation Stone of Moctezuma Xocoyotzin, Mexica (Aztec), about 1503}}

The stone presents the new ruler's accession as part of the cosmic order. The Mexica understood the world as the latest in a series of creations, each ending in catastrophe, and the carvings place Moctezuma in the present age as its rightful heir. Look for the date signs and cosmological symbols carved on its surfaces: they turn a political event into a moment in the life of the universe. Barely two decades later, the Spanish invasion would bring that world to a violent end.

## Continuities

It is important not to treat these cultures as vanished. Pueblo potters in the American Southwest continued long traditions into the nineteenth century and beyond, as the collection's **Parrot Olla** from the Pueblo of Acoma (about 1880s) shows, with its macaws, rainbows and flowers. Tairona communities in Colombia still perform masked dances that personify natural forces. The themes traced here, of water, growth and responsibility, remain living ideas.

When you explore this part of the collection, ask of each object where it comes from and which people made it. Then look for the recurring signs: water creatures, maize, speech scrolls, regalia and faces of authority. You will begin to see how different societies, independently and in their own visual languages, gave form to the same fundamental hopes.
