Vincent van Gogh's working life as a painter lasted roughly a decade, and most of the work we now know him for was made in the last few years of it. The collection holds eight of his works, and they happen to span that short career with unusual clarity: a dark, earthy scene from the Netherlands, a burst of experiment in Paris, and the intense, emotionally charged paintings of Arles and Saint-Rémy. Following them in order is one of the best ways to watch an artist invent himself.

## Nuenen: earth colours and "human nests"

Van Gogh did not start out as a colourist. *A Peasant Woman Digging in Front of Her Cottage* (c. 1885) was painted in Nuenen, the Brabant village where his parents lived and where he returned in 1883 after a lonely, impoverished stretch in The Hague. The palette is dominated by browns, dull greens and greys. The woman bends over the soil beside a low thatched house that seems to grow out of the ground.

His letters from this time talk about his feeling for cottages and their inhabitants as "human nests". The phrase fits the painting. Its subject is not the picturesque countryside but shelter, labour and poverty, themes he absorbed from admired painters of peasant life such as Jean-François Millet. What he lacked was not conviction but colour, and he would find that in Paris.

## Paris: the colour arrives

In 1886 Van Gogh moved to Paris to live with his brother Theo, an art dealer. Over the next two years he met the Impressionists and the younger painters around Georges Seurat and Paul Signac, and his work transformed.

The *Self-Portrait* of 1887 shows the change at its most concentrated. The face and background are built from short, separate dabs of bright colour, a technique borrowed from Seurat's pointillism but handled with a nervous, rhythmic energy entirely Van Gogh's own. The eyes anchor the whole field of strokes. He painted more than thirty-five self-portraits over his lifetime, many of them in Paris, partly because he could not afford models. Each one is also an experiment in colour.

The same year produced *Fishing in Spring, the Pont de Clichy (Asnières)*, painted along the Seine where he sometimes worked alongside Signac, and *Grapes, Lemons, Pears, and Apples*, a still life in which complementary colours and a pulsing, patterned tablecloth seem to create a force field around the fruit. *Terrace and Observation Deck at the Moulin de Blute-Fin, Montmartre* records the half-rural hill a short walk from the brothers' apartment, with its windmills and kitchen gardens, a landscape then vanishing under the expanding city.

## Arles: the bedroom

In February 1888 Van Gogh left for Arles in the south of France. He rented the Yellow House and dreamed of turning it into a "Studio of the South", where artists could live and work together. The painting that best expresses that dream is *The Bedroom*.

{{artwork:28560|wide|The Bedroom, 1889}}

Van Gogh painted his Arles bedroom three times. The first version dates from 1888; the canvas in the collection is one of the later repetitions, made in 1889. He described the aim of the composition as expressing complete rest, and yet many viewers find the room anything but restful. The floor tilts steeply toward us, the walls lean, and the colours, violet-blue walls, a yellow bed, a red coverlet, green window, clash with deliberate intensity.

Both things are true. Van Gogh wanted the simplicity of the furniture and the flat, unshaded colour, which he associated with Japanese woodblock prints, to soothe the eye and the mind. But the painting was made by someone who had recently been hospitalised, who had lost the house itself, and who was painting the room from memory and from his earlier canvas. What was once a picture of home became a picture of longing for it.

Look for the details that make the room personal: the paired chairs and pillows, the portraits hanging above the bed, the clothes on their pegs, the towel by the washstand.

> The Bedroom is at once a still life, a self-portrait without a figure, and a record of a hope that did not survive.

## The Roulins, Gauguin and reconciliation

Van Gogh's dream of an artists' colony produced exactly one guest. Paul Gauguin arrived in Arles in October 1888, and the two men worked side by side for about two months before their relationship collapsed in December, in the crisis that ended with Van Gogh injuring his own ear and being hospitalised.

*Madame Roulin Rocking the Cradle (La berceuse)* was completed early in 1889, soon after he left the hospital. Augustine Roulin was the wife of the postman Joseph Roulin, whose family had become Van Gogh's closest friends in Arles and, to him, a model of love and family life. She holds the rope of a cradle we cannot see, set against a flat, flowered wallpaper. Van Gogh painted several versions of the subject. This one shows her seated in the chair Gauguin had used, and Van Gogh later gave the canvas to Gauguin himself, a gesture of reconciliation after their break.

## Saint-Rémy: copying as consolation

In May 1889 Van Gogh entered the asylum at Saint-Rémy-de-Provence. There, often unable to work outdoors, he made painted "translations" of prints by artists he loved. *The Drinkers* (1890) reworks a composition after Honoré Daumier: four figures of different ages bent over their cups, which Van Gogh renders with a pathos that turns an illustration into something close to a parable about need.

Copying freed him from inventing compositions and let him pour everything into colour and handling. It is a useful reminder that even in his last year, Van Gogh thought of himself as working within a tradition, not outside it.

He died in July 1890 in Auvers-sur-Oise, aged thirty-seven. Viewed together, the works in the collection make one thing plain: the intensity people associate with his life is present in his paintings less as chaos than as discipline, a hard-won control of colour that took him only five years to master.
