Few paintings are as familiar, or as odd, as Georges Seurat's *A Sunday on La Grande Jatte — 1884*. It shows Parisians on an island in the Seine on a summer afternoon: couples strolling, a woman fishing, children playing, boats on the water, a monkey on a leash. It should be the most relaxed scene imaginable. Instead the figures seem frozen, arranged like pieces on a board, and the whole canvas hums with a strange, almost mechanical calm.

That tension between subject and method is what makes the picture worth a long look.

## A suburban island, a monumental canvas

La Grande Jatte was a long, narrow island on the Seine just northwest of Paris, reached easily from the city and popular with people of every class on their day off. Seurat chose it for his most ambitious painting, and he chose to make it enormous, about two by three metres, the scale traditionally reserved for history painting and grand mythological scenes.

That choice was a statement. Seurat was giving ordinary modern leisure the dignity and permanence of classical art. Many writers have compared the processional, profile figures to ancient friezes or Egyptian reliefs, and the comparison helps: nobody here is caught mid-gesture the way they would be in a quick Impressionist sketch. They are posed for eternity.

## The view from across the river

The collection also holds a work that shows where this project began. *Final Study for "Bathers at Asnières"* (1883) is a small oil on panel made for Seurat's first large painting. It looks at the opposite bank of the same stretch of river, at Asnières, a working-class suburb with factory chimneys on the horizon, where men and boys lounge and swim.

{{artwork:20199|inset}}

Set the two side by side and a deliberate contrast appears. At Asnières, the figures are workers resting in their shirtsleeves; across the water on La Grande Jatte, the crowd is dressed in its Sunday best, bustles and top hats included. Seurat seems to have conceived the two paintings as a pair, looking at the same afternoon from different sides of Parisian society.

The Asnières study also shows Seurat's working method before he fully developed the dotted technique. The brushwork is a crisscross of short strokes, still close to Impressionism. For *La Grande Jatte*, he made dozens of drawings and small oil sketches on the island, then composed the final painting in the studio, adjusting figures and their placement over a long period.

## How the dots work

Seurat's technique is usually called Pointillism, though he himself preferred more scientific-sounding terms. It grew out of his reading of colour theory, especially the ideas of the chemist Michel-Eugène Chevreul on "simultaneous contrast": the way colours placed next to each other change how we perceive them.

Rather than mixing a green on his palette, Seurat would place small touches of related and contrasting colours side by side on the canvas, intending the viewer's eye to combine them at a distance. His hope was that colours blended optically would look more luminous than colours blended physically.

> Up close, the grass is a field of separate touches in yellow, green, blue and orange. Step back a few metres and they merge into a single, shimmering lawn in afternoon sun.

Try this with the painting. Look at the shadow under the trees on the left: it is not a flat dark green but a mesh of blues, purples and greens, with flecks of orange where sunlight would be reflected. Look at the edges of the figures, too. Where a light form meets a dark one, Seurat often lightened the background next to the dark edge and darkened it next to the light one, sharpening the contrast in a way the eye accepts without noticing.

It is worth knowing that the painting has changed since Seurat finished it. Some of the pigments he used have shifted over time, so certain dots that were once bright have dulled. The effect he intended was, if anything, more vivid than what we see now.

## The painted border

One detail often goes unnoticed: the frame of dots around the image. Seurat added this painted border in 1888–89, a few years after the main composition was complete. It is made of the same small touches, and the colours change along its length to respond to the colours of the scene next to them, darker beside light areas and lighter beside dark ones.

The border is Seurat's attempt to control how the picture meets the world. A conventional gilded frame, he felt, interfered with the colour relationships inside the painting. By painting his own transition, he made the edge part of the optical system.

## Its afterlife

*La Grande Jatte* was shown at the eighth and final Impressionist exhibition in 1886, where it became the centre of attention and something of a manifesto for a new generation. Painters such as Paul Signac took up and extended the method, and for a period in Paris in 1887 even Vincent van Gogh experimented with it, as can be seen in his *Self-Portrait* and *Fishing in Spring, the Pont de Clichy (Asnières)*, both in the collection.

{{artwork:80607|inset}}

Seurat died in 1891 at just thirty-one, having completed only a handful of large paintings. It is remarkable how much this single canvas has absorbed of his short career. When you look at it, try doing two things in turn: stand close enough to see the dots as dots, and then far enough away that they vanish. The painting lives in the space between those two distances.
