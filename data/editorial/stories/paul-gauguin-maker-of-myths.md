Paul Gauguin is one of the most influential painters of the late nineteenth century and one of the hardest to write about simply. His flat planes of saturated colour and his refusal to copy nature opened doors for the next generation of modern artists. His life, and especially his years in French Polynesia, raise questions about colonialism, exploitation and fantasy that his admirers once ignored and that we can no longer avoid.

The collection is an excellent place to see both sides. It holds paintings from Brittany, Arles and Tahiti, but also a carved figurine, a decorated box, a ceramic vase and a collaborative relief, works that show Gauguin as a restless maker in many materials.

## From stockbroker to avant-garde

Gauguin came to art late. Through his twenties and early thirties he worked in the Paris financial world, painting and collecting on the side. He bought works by the Impressionists, befriended Camille Pissarro, and was invited to show with the group, which he did in several of their exhibitions in the early 1880s.

What stands out from these years is how early he wanted to cross boundaries between art forms. *Woman Strolling* (c. 1880) is a small wooden figurine that he exhibited at the Sixth Impressionist Exhibition in 1881. Critics dismissed it as crude; some fellow artists admired precisely that directness. *Decorated Wooden Box* (1884) goes further, combining dancers reminiscent of Edgar Degas on the front, Japanese netsuke masks set into the back, and, inside, a rough carved figure inspired by Bronze Age Danish coffins. Gauguin was already assembling a personal visual vocabulary from sources all over the world.

In the wake of the stock market crash of 1882 he committed himself to art full-time, a decision that brought years of financial hardship and strained his marriage and family life.

## Brittany, ceramics and Arles

In the later 1880s Gauguin worked in Pont-Aven and elsewhere in Brittany, where he and younger artists such as Émile Bernard developed a style of bold outlines and flat colour areas. *Earthly Paradise* (1888), a painted wooden relief the two made together, uses motifs of Breton village life and deliberately blurs the line between fine and decorative art.

The same impulse shaped his ceramics. Invited to work with the ceramist Ernest Chaplet, Gauguin modelled his own irregular vessels by hand rather than decorating ready-made pots. *Vase in the Form of a Tropical Plant with Bird and Deity* (1887–88) is typical: the body of the vase echoes the plant painted on it, and leaf-like projections suggest handles that serve no practical purpose. He jokingly referred to such pieces as "monstrosities", but they are among the most inventive objects he made.

In October 1888 he joined Vincent van Gogh in Arles. The stay lasted about two months and ended badly, but it was astonishingly productive. *Arlésiennes (Mistral)*, one of seventeen canvases he completed there, shows women of Arles in the public garden opposite Van Gogh's house. The figures are silhouettes, the space is ambiguous, the colours are unmodelled. Nothing is observed casually; every shape feels chosen.

## Tahiti: paradise imagined

In 1891 Gauguin sailed for Tahiti, then a French colony, hoping to find a society untouched by European modernity. He did not find it: the island had been shaped by decades of missionary activity and colonial administration. What he produced instead was a body of work that mixes observation with invention.

*Te raau rahi (The Big Tree)* (1891) and *Te burao (The Hibiscus Tree)* (1892) show him grappling with an unfamiliar landscape, more interested in the colour and texture of the plants than in describing them accurately. In *Mahana no atua (Day of the God)* (1894), painted during a return to Paris, the fantasy takes over completely: a deity presides over bathers and figures arranged in bands of acid pink, orange and blue that read more like an abstract pattern than a place.

This is the Gauguin who mattered so much to later painters: colour freed from description, composition driven by rhythm and symbol.

## Looking critically

The same paintings demand a harder look. *Merahi metua no Tehamana (Tehamana Has Many Parents)* (1893) portrays a thirteen-year-old Tahitian girl whom Gauguin took as a so-called wife. She wears a missionary dress and holds a fan; behind her, Gauguin combined glyphs derived from an Easter Island tablet with a female deity drawn from Polynesian and Hindu sources, building a generic image of the "exotic" rather than anything specific to her.

> Gauguin's Tahiti paintings tell us a great deal about European fantasies of the Pacific, and much less about the people who actually lived there.

Recognising this does not mean the paintings stop being powerful. It means looking at them as images made by a particular man, with particular privileges, in a colonial situation. Titles like *No te aha oe riri (Why Are You Angry?)* (1896) can be read as teasing and ambiguous; they can also be read as putting words into the mouths of women whose own perspectives the paintings rarely allow.

Gauguin returned to the Pacific in 1895 and spent his last years there, dying in 1903 in the Marquesas Islands. *Polynesian Woman with Children* (1901), one of his late works, shows figures who have never been identified; technical study shows he reworked their hands and added the cat to strengthen the composition's upward axis.

His legacy, for Matisse, Picasso and many others, was enormous. The fullest way to see his work in the collection is to hold both things at once: the radical invention of the artist, and the questions his subjects still ask of us.
