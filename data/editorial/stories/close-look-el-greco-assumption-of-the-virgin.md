When Doménikos Theotokópoulos arrived in Spain in the 1570s, he was a Greek painter in his mid-thirties with an unusual history and no major public work to his name. Within a couple of years he had produced *The Assumption of the Virgin*, a towering canvas, roughly four metres high, that announced a completely original artist. The Spanish would call him simply El Greco, "the Greek". This painting is where that name began to mean something.

{{artwork:87479|wide|The Assumption of the Virgin, 1577–79}}

## Three worlds behind one painter

El Greco's path to Toledo helps explain what we see. He trained first on Crete, then under Venetian rule, as a painter of icons in the Byzantine tradition: gold grounds, frontal figures, and images meant to make the sacred present rather than to imitate the visible world. Around 1567 he moved to Venice, where the great colourists, above all Titian, taught him to build form with loose, expressive brushwork and glowing colour. He then spent several years in Rome, absorbing the muscular, twisting figures of Michelangelo and the elegant distortions of the Mannerist painters.

By the time he reached Spain, he could draw on all three. The *Assumption* is the first large-scale proof of how he would fuse them.

## The commission

The painting was the central element of the main altarpiece for the church of Santo Domingo el Antiguo, a convent of Cistercian nuns in Toledo. It was El Greco's first major Spanish commission and his first large public work, and he was responsible not only for the paintings but for the design of the altarpiece frame as a whole. For an ambitious newcomer, it was an extraordinary opportunity, and he treated it accordingly.

The subject, the Assumption, is the belief that at the end of her earthly life the Virgin Mary was taken bodily into heaven. It was an especially important image in Catholic Spain in the decades after the Council of Trent, when the Church encouraged art that would move worshippers emotionally and affirm doctrines that Protestant reformers rejected.

## Reading the two halves

The composition splits cleanly into an earthly zone and a heavenly one.

**Below**, the apostles crowd around the empty stone tomb. They have come to find Mary's body and discovered it gone. Their reactions vary: some look up in wonder, some gesture toward the tomb, others turn to one another as if asking what has happened. The figures in the front are large and heavily draped, pushed right up to the picture's lower edge so that they seem to share our space. Notice how the draperies are painted, in broad sweeps of saturated red, green, gold and blue, with highlights laid on in quick, confident strokes. This is Venetian painting at full volume.

**Above**, Mary rises in a swirl of angels, her feet resting on a crescent moon, a traditional symbol of her purity. She is not climbing or flying so much as being carried, with her arms opening outward and her gaze lifted. The light around her is pale and luminous, and the clouds are dense enough to seem almost solid, forming a kind of platform between the two worlds.

> The genius of the composition is the gap in the middle. The apostles look up; we follow their eyes; and the painting turns our act of looking into the act of witnessing a miracle.

Compared with Titian's famous treatment of the subject in Venice, which El Greco surely knew, the Toledo picture is more compressed and more agitated. The figures fill almost every inch of the canvas; there is little breathing room. That density, combined with the bold colour, gives the scene its sense of urgency.

## What to look for

A few details reward slow looking:

- **The hands.** El Greco uses gesture as a language. Follow the hands across the lower half and they almost tell the story on their own, pointing, pleading, recoiling.
- **The faces.** Several apostles are clearly studied from life models, with individualised features, a reminder that this painting is still grounded in close observation.
- **The brushwork up close.** In the draperies and clouds, the paint is applied loosely enough that forms seem to vibrate. Modern artists, from Mary Cassatt to Pablo Picasso, admired this painting in part for exactly that freedom.

## Where El Greco went next

The *Assumption* is still relatively balanced and classical compared with what came later. The other works by El Greco in the collection show how far he would push his style over the following decades in Toledo.

{{artwork:67362|inset}}

In *Saint Martin and the Beggar* (c. 1597–1600), the saint, in fashionable armour, cuts his cloak to share it with a nearly naked beggar whose body is stretched to an elongated, almost flame-like height. The walls of Toledo appear in the distance, bringing the legend into the viewers' own city. *Saint Francis Kneeling in Meditation* (c. 1595–1600) pares the image down to a single figure, a skull, a book and a crucifix in a stormy landscape, inviting the viewer to meditate alongside the saint. *The Feast in the House of Simon* (c. 1608–14), a late work in which his workshop, likely including his son Jorge Manuel, had a hand, shows the elongated figures and flickering highlights that became the hallmarks of his mature manner.

{{artwork:21907|inset}}

Seen together, these paintings chart a movement away from the Venetian solidity of the *Assumption* toward something more spiritual and more personal, in which bodies stretch, light flickers and space becomes uncertain. That later style made El Greco seem out of step for centuries; around 1900, it made him look startlingly modern. The *Assumption* is where the journey begins, the moment a Cretan icon painter trained in Venice and Rome became the painter of Toledo.
