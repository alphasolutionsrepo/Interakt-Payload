Much of the finest painted pottery to survive from ancient Greece was made for one social ritual: the symposium, a drinking party held in a private house. Men reclined on couches around the room, a master of ceremonies set the strength of the wine, and the evening moved from toasts and songs to poetry, games, conversation and, not infrequently, excess. Every stage required a specialized vessel, and Greek potters and painters rose to the challenge with an extraordinary range of shapes and images.

The Greek vases in the collection offer a way into that world, and into the technical revolution that changed how those vessels were painted.

## A vessel for every stage

The Greeks almost never drank wine neat. It was diluted with water in a large mixing bowl, the **krater**, and ladled out into cups. Around the krater stood a family of related shapes:

- the **amphora** and **stamnos**, jars for storing or mixing wine;
- the **hydria**, a three-handled water jar;
- the **oinochoe**, a pitcher for pouring;
- the **kylix**, **skyphos** and **kantharos**, all drinking cups;
- the **rhyton**, a novelty cup, often shaped like an animal's head.

A red-figure **Column-Krater** of about 460 BCE shows the central vessel of the party. Its painted scene is a pointed choice for a drinking bowl: Zeus strikes down Salmoneus, a king who had arrogantly imitated the god's thunder and lightning. It is a story about overreaching, placed on the object that controlled how drunk the room would get.

## From black to red

Look at the collection's pottery in date order and you can watch a change in technique unfold. Earlier vessels are **black-figure**: figures painted in a glossy slip that fires black, with details scratched through to the orange clay beneath. The technique was developed at Corinth, and a Corinthian **Oinochoe** of 640–625 BCE shows its early vitality, with animals and fantastic beasts marching in bands around the body, touched with added purple-red and white.

{{artwork:641|inset}}

Athenian painters took black-figure to its peak in the sixth century. Then, around 530 BCE, they reversed the scheme. In **red-figure** painting the background is filled in with black and the figures are left in the colour of the clay, with interior details drawn in fine lines of slip. The effect was liberating. A brush could render the fall of drapery, the twist of a torso or the turn of a face more fluidly than an engraving tool scratching through a silhouette.

The **Stamnos** attributed to the Chicago Painter, around 450 BCE, shows the mature style. It depicts the women followers of Dionysos, god of wine, and it still has its original lid, a rare survival. The painter takes his modern name from this collection: scholars identify anonymous Athenian vase painters by their hand, and this artist was named after the first vessel of his to be recognized, acquired by the Art Institute of Chicago in 1889. His **Hydria**, showing three women with a mirror, a jewellery chest and a fruit, shows the same delicate line applied to domestic life.

## Cups that play games

The symposium valued wit, and its cups could be jokes in clay. The black-figure **Kylix** from the workshop of Nikosthenes (530–520 BCE) is an "eye cup". Painted with large staring eyes on the outside, it became a mask as soon as the drinker lifted it: the handles read as ears, the hollow underside of the foot as a mouth, and the drinker's own face vanished behind the gaze of Dionysos.

{{artwork:208235|inset}}

Other vessels made the drinker a participant in the fun. The red-figure **Rhyton in the Shape of a Donkey Head** (480–470 BCE) cannot be put down without spilling, so it had to be emptied in one go. Its neck shows a satyr chasing a maenad, and the donkey itself belongs to Dionysos's rowdy entourage. A later rhyton from Taranto takes the shape of a sheep's head, with the same mischievous logic. The collection also holds a **Kantharos in the Shape of a Female Head** (about 480 BCE), with faces on front and back, turning a wine cup into a small sculpture.

> Greek drinking vessels were not neutral containers. They talked back, told stories and set the tone of the evening.

## Beyond the party

The same workshops served other rituals too. Oil was essential in Greek life, for athletics, bathing and the care of the dead, and its containers carried their own imagery. The white-ground **Lekythos** attributed to the Reed Painter (410–400 BCE) was made for the grave. Its pale surface, painted after firing in colours that have largely faded, showed mourners visiting a tomb. It is a reminder that the same craft that produced exuberant party ware also produced quiet, tender objects of mourning.

By the fourth and third centuries BCE, workshops in the Greek cities of southern Italy had taken up the tradition. Apulian vessels in the collection, such as the grand red-figure **Volute Krater** of about 340 BCE and the colourful **Gnathia-ware kantharos**, with its added reds, yellows and whites over black gloss, show how the style kept evolving far from Athens.

## How to look at a Greek vase

When you study a pot, start with its shape and ask what it was for: pouring, mixing, drinking, storing. Then look at how the painting follows the form. Friezes wrap around shoulders, scenes are framed by handles, and a cup's interior often holds a surprise that would be revealed only as the wine went down. Finally, notice the technique: silhouette and incision, or open figures with brushed lines. Each vessel is a small collaboration between potter and painter, and a record of how the Greeks liked to spend an evening.
