Francisco de Goya is often remembered for his darkest work: the etchings of the *Disasters of War*, the nightmarish *Caprichos*, the "Black Paintings" he made on the walls of his own house late in life. Those images have shaped how we picture him, a lonely, deaf visionary staring into the violence of his age. The works by Goya in the collection tell a broader and in some ways more surprising story. They show a painter who could be decorative and charming, who was fascinated by ordinary Spanish life, and who, in one remarkable series, turned a newspaper-worthy crime into a sequence of little paintings that reads almost like a comic strip.

## A painter's rise

Goya was born in 1746 in Fuendetodos, a village near Zaragoza in Aragon. After training locally and a period in Italy, he moved to Madrid in the 1770s and began working for the Royal Tapestry Factory of Santa Bárbara, which produced wall hangings for the royal palaces. The job was not glamorous, but it was steady, and it put him in front of the court. Over the next two decades he rose to become one of the king's painters and, eventually, First Court Painter.

A serious illness in the early 1790s left him permanently deaf. Many scholars see a darkening and deepening of his art from that point on, as he turned increasingly to private works, prints and subjects of his own choosing. He lived through the French invasion of Spain and the brutal war that followed, and in his last years moved to Bordeaux in France, where he died in 1828.

## Designing for the king's dining room

The two earliest Goyas in the collection come from his tapestry work, and both relate to a series of hangings designed in the mid-1780s for a dining hall in the Royal Palace of El Pardo, outside Madrid. Goya's process involved two stages. First he made small oil sketches to work out the composition; then he painted full-scale "cartoons" that the weavers followed.

*Winter Scene* (c. 1786) is one of the preparatory studies, for a tapestry representing winter in a cycle of the four seasons. Two groups of travellers meet on an icy road in a snowstorm; one party has a slaughtered pig in tow. Goya evokes the cold with broad passages of grey and white, bare trees and diagonal strokes that drive the snow across the scene. Look at the huddled bodies wrapped in blankets and cloaks. Even in a decorative commission, he gives weather and hardship a physical reality.

*Boy on a Ram* (1786–87) is a full-scale cartoon from the same scheme. A child in fine clothes rides a ram through a landscape, a light, playful subject suited to a royal interior. It has the lightness and charm of the Rococo tradition that still shaped court taste, but the confident handling of paint and the direct observation of the animal show Goya's growing independence.

## The capture of El Maragato

Around 1806 Goya painted something entirely different: six small oil panels telling the story of a real event that had caused a sensation in Spain. A notorious bandit known as El Maragato had escaped from prison and spent weeks robbing travellers. When he tried to take hostages, including a friar named Pedro de Zaldivia, the friar tricked him, seized his gun and overpowered him. The story spread quickly through popular prints and pamphlets.

The collection holds all six panels. Read in sequence, they unfold like frames in a film:

1. *El Maragato Threatens Friar Pedro de Zaldivia with His Gun*
2. *Friar Pedro Offers Shoes to El Maragato and Prepares to Push Aside His Gun*
3. *Friar Pedro Wrests the Gun from El Maragato*
4. *Friar Pedro Clubs El Maragato with the Butt of the Gun*
5. *Friar Pedro Shoots El Maragato as His Horse Runs Off*
6. *Friar Pedro Binds El Maragato with a Rope*

{{artwork:16362|wide|Friar Pedro Shoots El Maragato as His Horse Runs Off, c. 1806}}

Goya kept the settings minimal, a bare interior, then an open landscape, so that everything depends on the two figures and their shifting power. The painting is quick and sketch-like, with figures built from a few decisive strokes and highlights. In the early panels, the bandit looms large and threatening; as the story progresses, the small, robed friar becomes increasingly dominant, until in the final scene he calmly ties up his captive while the bandit lies helpless.

> These panels treat a piece of popular news as worthy of serious painting, and they do it with the narrative drive of a picture story.

There is also humour here. The contrast between the burly criminal and the unassuming friar, and the absurd detail of the friar offering the bandit a pair of shoes as a distraction, gives the series the quality of a folk tale. Goya made paintings like these for his own pleasure or for a small circle of patrons, and that freedom shows in their energy.

## What connects them

At first glance, the delicate tapestry designs and the Maragato panels seem to belong to different artists. Look closer and common threads appear:

- **An eye for ordinary people.** Peasants in a snowstorm and a village friar are as interesting to Goya as courtiers.
- **Physical drama.** Whether it is wind and snow or a struggle over a gun, Goya makes you feel bodies under pressure.
- **Speed of handling.** Even in finished works, he lets the brush show, suggesting forms rather than describing every detail.

{{artwork:20692|inset}}

These qualities would come to the fore in the unsettling works of his later years. Seen in the collection, Goya's range is the real revelation: the same painter who designed a charming boy on a ram for a royal dining room could, twenty years later, turn a bandit's capture into one of the liveliest narrative series of his time.
