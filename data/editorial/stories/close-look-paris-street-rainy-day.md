Some paintings you look at; *Paris Street; Rainy Day* is one you seem to step into. Gustave Caillebotte's large canvas of 1877 places us on the pavement of a Paris intersection on a wet afternoon, close enough to a strolling couple that we might have to step aside for them. It is one of the defining images of the modern city, and it rewards the kind of attention you might give a crowded street: noticing who is there, how they move, and what the buildings are doing to all of them.

## A city remade

The Paris in this painting was new. From the 1850s onward, under Napoleon III and his prefect Baron Haussmann, large parts of the old medieval city were demolished and replaced with broad, straight boulevards, uniform apartment blocks of pale limestone, and open intersections designed for traffic, light and air. The renovation took decades and reshaped daily life in the capital.

Caillebotte chose a spot that captured that transformation at its most characteristic: a star-shaped crossing in the recently built quarter around the Gare Saint-Lazare, where several streets meet at sharp angles. The wedge-shaped building in the middle distance, its prow pointing at us, is the kind of architecture the new street plan produced. Everything is clean, geometric and orderly. Even the paving stones, glistening with rain, are laid out in a grid.

## How the composition works

At first glance the painting looks like a straightforward, almost photographic view. Look longer and you see how carefully it has been constructed.

- **The lamppost.** A tall green lamppost rises from the pavement near the centre, dividing the canvas almost exactly in half. On the right, the large figures are close to us; on the left, the space opens out into the deep perspective of the intersection.
- **The perspective.** The streets recede sharply, and the buildings are rendered with crisp precision. Caillebotte, who had trained in law before turning to painting, approached the city with an almost architectural rigour.
- **The cropping.** A man on the right edge is cut off mid-stride, and figures in the distance are sliced by other figures and umbrellas. This is not the balanced framing of academic painting but the partial, accidental view of someone walking in a real street.

The result is a painting that feels both carefully posed and spontaneously glimpsed, and that combination is a large part of its fascination.

## Fashion, umbrellas and strangers

Caillebotte dressed his figures in the latest styles. The woman in the foreground wears a veil, pearl earring and fur-trimmed coat; the man beside her wears a top hat and a well-cut overcoat. These are bourgeois Parisians who would have recognised themselves in the painting. Their umbrellas, of a design that had recently become widely available, are almost as important as the people. They repeat across the canvas like a pattern, creating a rhythm of dark domes above the grey pavement.

They also do something subtler. Umbrellas create private space in public. Each walker is sheltered, separated from the others by a small moving roof. Nobody in the painting meets anyone else's eye. The couple in the foreground look toward something outside the frame; the other pedestrians are absorbed in their own errands.

> The painting is full of people and yet strangely quiet. It captures a distinctly modern experience: being surrounded by others and fundamentally alone.

This is where Caillebotte's view of the city differs from the sunlit bustle of many Impressionist street scenes. His Paris is elegant and prosperous, but also cool and reserved, a place of anonymous encounters on well-ordered streets.

## Caillebotte among the Impressionists

Caillebotte was younger than Monet, Renoir and Degas, and wealthier. He supported the group financially, helped organise their exhibitions, and bought their works. *Paris Street; Rainy Day* was the centrepiece of his contribution to the third Impressionist exhibition in 1877.

Stylistically, though, it stands apart. Its smooth finish and precise drawing are closer to academic realism than to Monet's broken brushwork. What makes it Impressionist is its subject, the fleeting moment of modern urban life, and its interest in weather: the damp light, the reflections on wet stone, the muted colour of an overcast day.

It is illuminating to compare it with another view of the same neighbourhood made the same year. Monet's *Arrival of the Normandy Train, Gare Saint-Lazare* (1877) looks inside the railway station a short walk away, dissolving iron, glass and steam into a haze of colour.

## A wider view of modern life

Other works in the collection help round out the picture of the modern city that Caillebotte and his colleagues were exploring. Edgar Degas's *The Millinery Shop* looks at the world of fashion from behind the counter, at the women who made the hats that Caillebotte's strollers wore. Édouard Manet's *Woman Reading* shows an elegant Parisian absorbed in a magazine, a glass of beer at hand, a scene staged to look like an outdoor café. Camille Pissarro's *The Crystal Palace* (1871) records a modern glass-and-iron landmark in suburban London.

Caillebotte himself did not paint only boulevards. *Calf's Head and Ox Tongue* (c. 1882), probably inspired by a butcher's shop below his family home, turns the raw material of the city's kitchens into an unsettling still life rendered in soft, almost decorative colour.

{{artwork:154121|inset}}

Returning to *Paris Street; Rainy Day* after these works, its ambition becomes clearer. It is not simply a pleasant scene of a rainy afternoon. It is a painting about what the new Paris looked like, how people moved through it, and how they held themselves apart within it. More than a century later, anyone who has walked through a city in the rain under an umbrella knows exactly how it feels.
