In the second half of the nineteenth century, an ambitious American painter almost had to go to Europe. Paris offered the best training, London and Paris the most prestigious exhibitions, and Italy, Spain and the Netherlands the old masters. Yet going abroad raised a question that each artist answered differently: what, if anything, made an American painter American?

James McNeill Whistler, John Singer Sargent and Winslow Homer offer three very different answers. The collection holds a substantial group of works by each, which makes it possible to follow their paths side by side.

## Whistler: art for art's sake

Whistler was born in Lowell, Massachusetts, in 1834, spent part of his childhood in Russia, and after a short, unsuccessful stint at the United States Military Academy at West Point went to Paris to study art. He settled in London in 1859 and never lived in America again.

His early *Grey and Silver: Old Battersea Reach* (1863) takes an industrial stretch of the Thames, with barges, workers and smoking chimneys, and unifies it with a restrained palette of browns and greys. The subject is realist; the treatment already points toward something else. By *Trouville (Grey and Green, the Silver Sea)* (1865), painted on the French coast where he worked alongside Gustave Courbet, the narrative has almost gone. A high horizon and broad bands of muted colour recall the Japanese woodblock prints he was collecting.

That fascination is on full display in *The Artist in His Studio* (1865–66), where Whistler presents himself, palette in hand, surrounded by Japanese scrolls and Chinese porcelain. He painted in thin layers of muted colour, seeking the flat, decorative harmony he admired in Asian art.

The climax of this development came in the 1870s with his *Nocturnes*. In *Nocturne: Blue and Gold—Southampton Water* (1872), an inlet on the English coast nearly disappears into evening. A few lights and the ghost of a shoreline float in a field of deep blue.

{{artwork:56905|wide|Nocturne: Blue and Gold—Southampton Water, 1872}}

Whistler borrowed musical terms, nocturne, harmony, arrangement, to insist that a painting was an arrangement of colour and form first, and a picture of something second. This "art for art's sake" position made him famous and controversial; it also led, in 1878, to his libel suit against the critic John Ruskin, who had attacked one of his nocturnes. Whistler won, but was awarded only a token farthing in damages. The same principle shapes his portraits: the *Study for "Arrangement in Grey and Black, No. 2: Portrait of Thomas Carlyle"* in the collection treats the historian's black coat as a shape in a carefully balanced design.

## Sargent: the cosmopolitan virtuoso

Sargent was, if anything, even less American by upbringing. He was born in Florence in 1856 to American parents who moved restlessly around Europe, and he did not visit the United States until he was twenty. He trained in Paris under the fashionable portraitist Carolus-Duran and travelled to study Velázquez in Spain and Frans Hals in the Netherlands.

The collection's early Sargents show him absorbing these lessons. *Venetian Glass Workers* (1880–82) is a dark interior lit only by silvery strokes describing canes of glass, a study in the kind of light and shadow he had seen in Spanish painting. *Madame Paul Escudier* (1882) carries that dramatic lighting into a Parisian apartment, undercutting conventional portraiture. *Rehearsal of the Pasdeloup Orchestra at the Cirque d'Hiver* (c. 1879) proves he could handle modern urban subjects with Impressionist economy.

{{artwork:4749|inset}}

His reputation, though, rested on society portraits. In *Mrs. George Swinton* (1897), the face is rendered with careful realism while the gown explodes into bravura strokes of shimmering paint. Sargent settled in London in 1886 and became the most sought-after portraitist on both sides of the Atlantic, painting British aristocrats and American industrialists alike. Around 1907 he largely stepped away from formal portraiture to paint outdoors; *The Fountain, Villa Torlonia, Frascati, Italy* (1907), showing his friends and fellow painters Wilfrid and Jane de Glehn beside a garden fountain, captures that turn toward light, colour and leisure.

## Homer: going abroad to come home

Homer's story runs the other way. Born in Boston in 1836, he began as an illustrator and made his name with images of the Civil War. His early oils, like *Croquet Scene* (1866), show fashionable Americans in bright sunlight. Shortly afterward he spent some months in Paris, where he could see the new French painting firsthand, but he never trained there and did not stay.

His decisive time abroad came later, in 1881–82, when he lived in the fishing village of Cullercoats on England's North Sea coast. Watching the fishing families there, he turned away from leisure and toward the struggle between people and the sea. Back in the United States, he settled in 1883 at Prouts Neck, Maine, where he would spend the rest of his life.

*The Herring Net* (1885) is the result: two fishermen in a small boat haul in a glittering catch amid heaving swells, their figures monumental and anonymous. By *Coast of Maine* (1893), even the people are gone. Surf crashes against dark rocks under a heavy sky, and the painting becomes a direct encounter with the force of nature.

> Whistler made Europe his home, Sargent made it his stage, and Homer used it as a lens to see America more clearly.

## What made them American?

Critics in their own time liked to call Homer the most American of painters, and there is truth in that: his subjects were American and his manner seemed plain and direct. Yet *Peach Blossoms* (1878), with its calligraphic tree and lightened palette, shows he too was absorbing Impressionism and Japanese art. Whistler and Sargent, for their part, never stopped calling themselves American, even as they built their careers in Europe.

Perhaps the best answer is that American art in this period was not a single style but a set of conversations with Europe. Walking through these three artists' works in the collection, you can hear each voice clearly: Whistler's quiet harmonies, Sargent's dazzling brush, and Homer's weathered, elemental seas.
