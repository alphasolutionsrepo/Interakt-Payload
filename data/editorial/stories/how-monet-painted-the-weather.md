Most landscape painters before Claude Monet treated weather as scenery, a backdrop that told you what season it was or set a mood behind the real subject. Monet turned that arrangement upside down. In his mature work, the field, the bridge or the cliff is little more than a surface for catching light, and what actually changes from canvas to canvas is the air: its temperature, its moisture, its colour at a particular minute of the day.

The collection holds nearly thirty paintings by Monet, from a breezy beach of 1867 to the water gardens he painted during the First World War. Taken together they read almost like a diary of skies. This essay follows that diary, and suggests what to look for when you stand, or scroll, in front of one.

## Learning to paint outdoors

Monet grew up in Le Havre on the Normandy coast, where the Channel weather changes by the hour. His early *The Beach at Sainte-Adresse* (1867) was begun outside and finished in the studio, and technical study has shown that it started life as a beach of yachts and tourists before becoming a scene of fishermen and working boats. Even at this stage the real drama is overhead: a broad, bright sky that takes up much of the canvas and sets the key for every colour below it.

A year later, in *On the Bank of the Seine, Bennecourt* (1868), the reflections in the river are laid in with loose, flat patches that many viewers at the time found shockingly unfinished. That "unfinish" was the point. Reflections on moving water never hold still, and a brushstroke that tries to fix them in detail tells a kind of lie. Monet's broken touch was a way of being truthful about how quickly light moves.

## Bad weather as an opportunity

It is tempting to imagine the Impressionists waiting for sunny days. Monet did the opposite. In the autumn of 1885 at Étretat, rain drove him indoors, and he painted *Boats on the Beach at Étretat* and its companion, *The Departure of the Boats*, from the window of his hotel room. Look closely at the hulls and shingle: they are built from pinks, lilacs, pale greens and blues, the colours of wet surfaces reflecting a grey sky. A lesser painter would have reached for brown.

The following year, on Belle-Île off the coast of Brittany, he planned a two-week stay and remained more than two months, working against wind and surf in *Rocks at Port-Goulphar*. On the Italian Riviera in 1884 the challenge was the reverse: a Mediterranean glare so strong that, in *Bordighera*, the sea glimmers through a lattice of pine trunks as if the light itself were too much to look at directly.

## The series: one motif, many skies

The great turning point came in 1890–91, when Monet began painting the stacks of wheat that stood in a field outside his farmhouse at Giverny. The collection holds a remarkable group of these canvases, and seeing them side by side is the clearest possible lesson in what Monet was after.

{{artwork:16560|wide|Stack of Wheat (Snow Effect, Overcast Day), 1890–91}}

The stacks themselves barely change. What changes is everything around them:

- In *Stacks of Wheat (End of Day, Autumn)*, the conical tops break the horizon and push into a warm, glowing sky.
- In *Stack of Wheat (Snow Effect, Overcast Day)*, the stack seems tucked into bands of hill and field, its shadow a cool violet on blue-white snow.
- In *Stacks of Wheat (Sunset, Snow Effect)* and *Stack of Wheat (Thaw, Sunset)*, low sun sets the edges of the forms burning orange while the shadows turn deep blue.

Monet worked on several canvases at once in the field, switching easels as the light shifted, and then refined the colour harmonies back in the studio so that each picture held together as a finished work. That second stage matters. These are not snapshots; they are carefully orchestrated paintings about fleeting conditions.

> The subject of a Monet series is not the haystack, the river or the bridge. It is the envelope of light and air that sits between the painter and the thing he is looking at.

He repeated the method again and again. For *Mornings on the Seine* (1897), represented in the collection by *Branch of the Seine near Giverny (Mist)*, he worked from a flat-bottomed boat at dawn, keeping numbered canvases in grooves so that his assistant could hand him the right one as the mist thinned. In 1901, from a rented balcony opposite Vétheuil, he painted the same view of the riverbank and church at different hours; the collection holds two of them.

## London fog

Monet's most extreme weather paintings came from London. Beginning in 1899 he made close to a hundred views of the Thames from just three vantage points: Waterloo and Charing Cross bridges from the Savoy Hotel, and the Houses of Parliament from St Thomas's Hospital across the river. The smoky, damp air of the industrial city, which many visitors found oppressive, was exactly what attracted him.

{{artwork:103139}}

Compare *Waterloo Bridge, Gray Weather* (1900) with *Waterloo Bridge, Sunlight Effect* (1903). The arches, the traffic and the chimneys beyond are the same, but in one they dissolve into a muffled blue-grey haze, while in the other the fog is shot through with warm, broken light. In *Houses of Parliament, London*, the Gothic towers become a single dark silhouette against a sky that seems to be burning. Monet finished these canvases back at Giverny, working on them as an ensemble until they satisfied him as a group, a reminder that his "impressions" were often the product of years of labour.

## What to look for

When you next look at a Monet in the collection, try setting aside the question of what is depicted and ask instead:

- **What time of day is it?** Warm oranges and long violet shadows usually mean early morning or late afternoon.
- **How wet is the air?** Mist and fog soften edges and push every colour toward a single tone.
- **Where is the light coming from?** Often the brightest touches are not white at all, but pale yellow, pink or mint green.

By the time he was painting his water lily pond in the 1910s, Monet had almost abolished the horizon. Sky appears only as a reflection on the water's surface. It is a fitting end point for a painter who spent his life proving that the weather was worth painting for its own sake.
