Many of the African works in the collection share a quality that is easy to feel but harder to name. They seem to be doing something. A figure bristles with blades; a head stares out with gleaming metal eyes; a mask, even at rest, looks ready to move. That impression is accurate. Most of these objects were made not simply to be looked at but to act: to protect, heal, judge, commemorate, and connect the living with ancestors and spiritual forces.

This essay looks at a small group of works from West and Central Africa and the Swahili Coast, and at how form, material and use come together in each.

## Sculptures that work

The most commanding of them is a **Male Figure (Nkisi Nkondi)**, made by a Vili artist of the Kongo cultural region, in what is now the Republic of the Congo, probably in the early to mid-nineteenth century. An *nkisi* is an object invested with spiritual power. That power comes from *bilongo*, medicinal and magical ingredients, which here are sealed in the cap on the figure's head and in a resin packet on its abdomen, closed with a mirror.

{{artwork:151358|wide|Male Figure (Nkisi Nkondi), Vili, Kongo region, probably early to mid-19th century}}

The term *nkondi* refers to the figure's role as a hunter of wrongdoers. When a person sought justice, swore an oath or sealed an agreement, a ritual specialist activated the figure by driving in a nail or blade. Each piece of metal is therefore a record of a specific appeal or promise. Look at the materials listed for this work: wood, metal, glass, fabric, fibre, cowrie shells, bone, leather, gourd and feathers. The figure is an accumulation. It gathered its present appearance over time, through use by a community.

## Guarding the ancestors

A very different presence comes from a **Head** made by a Fang artist in Gabon in the mid to late nineteenth century. It once sat on the lid of a cylindrical bark box holding the skull and bones of an honoured ancestor. Kept in a man's sleeping room, the box and its sculpture guarded the relics and kept the ancestor's power close to his descendants.

{{artwork:187528|inset}}

The Fang head is a study in controlled form: a domed forehead, a calm, compact face, and eyes inset with copper alloy that would catch the light in a dark room and startle an intruder. Its stillness is purposeful. The sculpture expresses the composure and authority expected of an ancestor, and serves as a watchful guardian.

## Masks, motherhood and the idea of beauty

The **Male Face Mask (Chihongo)** from the Chokwe peoples of Angola or the Democratic Republic of the Congo is the male counterpart to the celebrated female *mwana pwo* mask. Chokwe masquerades engage the ancestors, who are asked to guide, protect and teach the living. Every element of the chihongo carries meaning: the red colouring, scarification patterns, stylized beard, filed teeth and headdress of turaco and guinea-fowl feathers all signal wisdom, leadership and status. It is worth remembering that a mask in a collection is only part of a larger work. In performance it was joined by a costume, music, movement and an audience.

Ideas of beauty and goodness are central to many of these works. A **Mother-and-Child Figure (Bwanga bwa Chibola)** by a Luluwa sculptor from the Democratic Republic of the Congo expresses the Luluwa concept of *bwimpe*, which unites physical beauty with moral goodness. The delicate scarification, hairstyle and jewellery are not decoration for its own sake; they are signs of a well-lived, socially valued life. Such figures celebrated fertility and may have held medicine.

A **Female Figure with Bowl** by Abogunde of Ede, an acclaimed Yoruba sculptor active in the late nineteenth century, was made for a shrine to one of the *orisa*, possibly Shango, associated with thunder, warriors and kings. The figure is at once an offering, a container for offerings and an image of a devotee making an offering. It is also a reminder that African sculptures were made by individual artists with recognizable styles, even when their names were not recorded by the collectors who later acquired their work.

## Kingship and memory

That point is made even more clearly by the **Portrait Figure of Metang, the 10th King of Batufam**, carved around 1912–14 by the eastern Bangwa artist Mbeudjang for the Bamileke kingdom of Batufam in the Cameroon Grassfields.

{{artwork:148330|inset}}

Though called a portrait, it does not attempt a physical likeness. It presents an idealized image of kingship, and such commemorative figures were valued partners to rulers in protecting their realm and people. Traces of chalk and camwood on its surface point to ritual attention over time.

> In many African traditions a sculpture is less a picture of power than a participant in it.

## Deep histories, wide connections

The collection also reaches back centuries. A terracotta **Equestrian and Four Figures** from the Bankoni area of present-day Mali, probably late twelfth to fifteenth century, shows a rider with a knife strapped to his arm among attendants whose hairstyles and marks suggest status. It recalls the role of cavalry in the empires that controlled West African trade routes.

Other works reflect exchange across oceans. A nineteenth-century **Chair (Kiti Cha Enzi)** from Lamu on the Swahili Coast, made of wood, ivory and cotton fibre, draws together influences from centuries of trade with the Persian Gulf and western India and from Portuguese, Omani and British rule into a distinctly Swahili seat of honour. A Kongo **basketwork container** recalls a trade in luxury goods that reached European royal collections as early as the seventeenth century. A Zulu **headrest** protected elaborate hairstyles during sleep while abstractly evoking a bull, a sign of wealth and a link to the ancestors.

Finally, when you explore these works, look for signs of use and accumulation, such as nails, residues, repairs and added materials. Ask what the object was meant to do and for whom. And remember that each culture named here, Vili, Fang, Chokwe, Luluwa, Yoruba, Bamileke and Swahili among them, has its own history, language and artistic tradition. The collection offers only a glimpse, but it is a glimpse of art made with great purpose.
