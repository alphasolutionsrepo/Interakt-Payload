In the years just before and after the First World War, a handful of European painters took one of the most radical steps in the history of art. They let go of the depicted world. Instead of painting a landscape, a figure or a bowl of fruit, they made paintings whose subject was colour, line and form themselves. Two of the pioneers, Vasily Kandinsky and Piet Mondrian, are represented in the collection by works that allow us to follow their journeys step by step. What is striking is how differently they travelled, and how similar their goals turned out to be.

## Kandinsky: colour as sound

Vasily Kandinsky (1866–1944) came to painting relatively late, after training in law in Moscow, and settled in Munich. There he became a founding member of the Blaue Reiter (Blue Rider), a loose alliance of artists who believed that colours and forms had a spiritual and emotional power of their own, and who looked for inspiration to folk art, medieval art and art from outside Europe.

**Painting with Troika**, dated January 18, 1911, shows him in transition. Painted on cardboard and set in a frame the artist painted himself, it still contains recognizable motifs: a three-horse sleigh, the troika of Russian folk tales and memory, rushing across a landscape. But the forms are dissolving. Colour spills beyond its outlines, and the horses become arcs and dashes of energy.

Two years later, **Improvisation No. 30 (Cannons)** (1913) goes much further. In his book *Concerning the Spiritual in Art* (1912), Kandinsky argued for a painting that would move beyond imitation and act directly on the viewer's inner life, as music does. Between 1910 and 1914 he painted a series of "Improvisations", which he described as spontaneous expressions of inner feeling. The musical term is deliberate. In this canvas, colours clash and swirl across the surface. Yet a few images still surface: at lower right, the cannons of the title fire puffs of smoke, and buildings tilt as if shaken. Painted in 1913, on the eve of the war, the work can seem prophetic, though Kandinsky himself resisted reading it too literally.

{{artwork:8991|wide|Improvisation No. 30 (Cannons), 1913, by Vasily Kandinsky}}

Kandinsky's example resonated widely. His Blaue Reiter colleague Franz Marc painted **The Bewitched Mill** (1913), where the houses and mill on the left meet a lyrical zone of trees and animals, all bound together in strong, non-naturalistic colour. The American Marsden Hartley, working in Berlin, painted **Movement** (1913) in the same year, a canvas that aims, like Kandinsky's work, at the condition of music.

## Mondrian: the truth of the straight line

Piet Mondrian (1872–1944) arrived at abstraction by a completely different route: not through colour set free but through structure distilled. He began as a landscape painter in the Netherlands, and the flat Dutch countryside, with its horizon lines, canals and trees, stayed with him even after he left recognizable subjects behind.

**Farm near Duivendrecht** (about 1916) shows a motif he returned to again and again. He first sketched this farm, set behind trees and reflected in water, around 1905, but many of the related works, of which about twenty are known, date from the years of the First World War, when he was already experimenting with abstraction. It is as if he kept returning to a familiar place to test what he had learned: the horizontals of the water and ground, the verticals of the trunks, the balance between them.

{{artwork:144467|inset}}

In 1917 Mondrian joined the Dutch movement De Stijl (The Style), and developed the vocabulary for which he is famous: an off-white ground divided by black lines, with a few blocks of primary colour. **Lozenge Composition with Yellow, Black, Blue, Red, and Gray** (1921) turns a square canvas on its point so that it hangs as a diamond. The lines inside remain strictly horizontal and vertical, but the edges of the canvas are diagonal, and the grid appears to continue beyond the frame. The effect is both stable and dynamic.

**Composition (No. 1) Gray-Red** (1935) comes from his mature years, when he lived in Paris. As its title suggests, the palette is pared down to grey and red, and the black lines carry the composition. What looks simple is the result of long adjustment: Mondrian constantly repositioned lines and altered the width of bands in search of exact equilibrium.

> Kandinsky freed colour to sound like music. Mondrian stripped form down to find a lasting order. Both believed abstraction could speak a universal language.

## Why abstraction?

For both artists, abstraction was not an escape from meaning but a way to reach a deeper one. Kandinsky, influenced by spiritual thought, hoped painting could produce "vibrations in the soul". Mondrian believed that a balanced relation of straight lines and pure colours could express a universal harmony, and after the devastation of the war, he and his De Stijl colleagues hoped such art might contribute to a more harmonious society.

Their paths are almost opposite. One is intuitive, turbulent and colourful; the other is disciplined, calm and reduced. Yet both began with the visible world, a sleigh in the snow, a farm by the water, and both arrived at pictures whose subject is the act of seeing and feeling itself.

## How to look

When you study these works in the collection, try looking at them in chronological order. With Kandinsky, hunt for the last traces of objects, such as horses, cannons and hills, and notice how colour escapes them. With Mondrian, follow the lines to the edges of the canvas and ask whether the composition stops there or seems to continue. Then step back, and let the painting work the way both artists hoped it would: directly, without needing to be a picture of anything at all.
