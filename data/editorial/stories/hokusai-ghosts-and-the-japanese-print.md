In the West, Katsushika Hokusai (1760–1849) is known above all for a wave: the towering breaker of *Under the Wave off Kanagawa*, reproduced on everything from posters to phone cases. The collection offers a different, stranger Hokusai. Four prints from his series *One Hundred Ghost Tales* (*Hyaku monogatari*), published in 1831–32, show the artist in a macabre and playful mood. Alongside works by Utagawa Hiroshige and Kawanabe Kyōsai, they open a window onto the woodblock print as a popular medium of storytelling, spectacle and delight.

## A print for everyone

Japanese woodblock prints of the Edo period (1615–1868) were commercial products. A publisher commissioned a design from an artist, a carver cut it into blocks of cherry wood, one for each colour, and a printer rubbed the colours onto paper by hand, sheet after sheet. Prints were affordable, fashionable and topical. They advertised kabuki actors, celebrated beauties of the pleasure quarters, guided armchair travellers along famous roads and, as here, retold stories that everyone already knew.

That shared knowledge matters. A print could be compact because its audience filled in the rest. When Hokusai's customers saw a skeleton peering over a mosquito net, they recognized the story at once.

## One hundred ghost tales

The title of Hokusai's series refers to a popular summer pastime. Friends would gather at night, light a hundred lamps or candles and take turns telling ghost stories, extinguishing one light after each tale, until the room was dark and, it was said, something might appear. Only a handful of designs from Hokusai's series were ever issued, which makes the four in the collection especially valuable as a group.

{{artwork:47398|inset}}

- **Kohada Koheiji** shows the ghost of a kabuki actor who was drowned by his wife's lover. The skeletal figure pulls down the mosquito net to stare at the couple in bed. Hokusai makes the horror intimate: the bones are drawn with fine, almost anatomical care, and the net becomes a fragile barrier between the living and the dead.
- **Oiwa** comes from the ghost play *Yotsuya Kaidan*. Poisoned and disfigured through a rival's scheme, Oiwa dies and returns for revenge. Hokusai fuses her face with a torn paper lantern, the rips becoming her features. The lantern's inscription asks the viewer to pray for her soul.
- **The Mansion of the Plates** (*Sara yashiki*) illustrates the tale of a woman killed and thrown into a well over broken dishes, whose ghost returns each night. Hokusai turns her into a long, serpentine neck of stacked plates rising from the well.
- **The Laughing Demoness** (*Warai Hannya*) shows a horned demon holding a child's severed head. It refers to the Buddhist figure known in Japan as Kishimojin, an ogress who devoured children until the Buddha brought her to understand a parent's grief, and who became a protector of children.

{{artwork:47403|wide|Oiwa, from the series One Hundred Ghost Tales, 1831–32, by Katsushika Hokusai}}

Notice the colour in these small prints. The vivid blue of *The Mansion of the Plates* may look cheerful to modern eyes, but Hokusai's audience associated it with death and the uncanny. The blue pigment itself was a novelty: synthetic Prussian blue had recently become widely available in Japan, and Hokusai and his contemporaries used it to striking effect.

> Hokusai's ghosts are frightening, but they are also witty. Each one is a visual pun, turning a lantern, a stack of plates or a mosquito net into a monster.

## Hiroshige's Edo after dark

A generation younger, Utagawa Hiroshige (1797–1858) is the great poet of place in the Japanese print. His late series *One Hundred Famous Views of Edo* (1856–58) revisits the city that is now Tokyo through the seasons. Two sheets in the collection show his range.

**The Pine of Success and Oumayagashi, Asakusa River** (1856) is a scene of dusk on the Sumida River. Stars are emerging, and a roofed boat drifts with its blinds lowered, a woman's silhouette just visible. The clue that she has company is tiny: two pairs of clogs in the prow. The pine was a landmark for visitors heading to the Yoshiwara pleasure district.

{{artwork:26617|inset}}

**New Year's Eve Fox Fires at the Changing Tree, Ōji** (1857) is unusual for Hiroshige because it is drawn from folklore. On the last night of the year, foxes, messengers of the Inari deity, were said to gather beneath a great tree near the Ōji Inari Shrine before visiting it, and the ghostly flames they produced were read by farmers as omens for the coming harvest. Hiroshige sets the glowing foxes against a deep night sky, a scene at once festive and eerie.

## After the Edo period

Japan opened to the West in the 1850s and 1860s, and prints changed with it. Kawanabe Kyōsai's triptych **May: Shōki the Demon Queller Riding on a Tiger** (1887) shows a traditional protective figure, often displayed around the Boys' Day festival in May, in a burst of energy as he and his tiger subdue a gang of goblins. The word "May" is printed in English along the edge, a small sign of a culture absorbing new influences while holding onto its old heroes.

## What to look for

When you explore the Japanese prints in the collection, look closely at the **line**, cut by the carver to follow the artist's brush, and at **colour**, especially gradations printed by wiping the block unevenly. Look for **seals and cartouches** that carry the series title and artist's signature. And look for the **story**: in many prints the key detail is small, like a pair of clogs or a line of text on a lantern, and it rewards the patient viewer.

The collection places these prints within a much longer East Asian tradition, from Shang dynasty ritual bronzes to Korean celadons and Chinese Buddhist sculpture. But the woodblock print holds a special place. It was art made for ordinary people, and it has kept its power to surprise, amuse and occasionally send a shiver down the spine.
