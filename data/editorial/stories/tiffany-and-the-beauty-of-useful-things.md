Around 1900, a generation of designers on both sides of the Atlantic shared a conviction: the objects people live with every day deserve as much artistic care as paintings on a wall. A lamp, a sideboard, a coffee pot or a fireplace could be beautiful, and a home could be designed as a unified work of art. The collection holds a compact but telling group of these objects. These notes offer a route through them, starting with the most famous name in American decorative art.

## Two Tiffanys

The name Tiffany covers two related enterprises. **Tiffany and Company**, the New York silver and jewellery firm founded in 1837 by Charles Lewis Tiffany, was already celebrated by the 1870s. His son, **Louis Comfort Tiffany**, trained as a painter and went on to lead the studios that made glass windows, lamps and interiors.

The firm's silver in the collection shows the ambition of the older business. In 1873 the United States government commissioned from Tiffany and Company a **punch bowl**, **wine coolers** and **candelabra** as a gift for the Brazilian diplomat Viscount d'Itajubá, in gratitude for his role in arbitrating between the United States and Great Britain after the Civil War. Chased with classical figures and emblems of agriculture and commerce, they were statements of national confidence in silver. Two decades later, the firm's **Saracenic Coffee Pot and Sugar Bowl** (1895), attributed to its chief silver designer Edward C. Moore, draws on Islamic metalwork, with a spherical body, slender neck and long spout. Moore collected Middle Eastern objects and design books, and his work reflects the late-nineteenth-century appetite for forms from other cultures.

## Light through glass

Louis Comfort Tiffany's great material was glass. He developed Favrile glass, an opalescent glass with colour running through it, so that shading, texture and depth came from the material itself rather than from paint applied to its surface.

{{artwork:192781|wide|Lilies (Corey Memorial Window), Tiffany Glass and Decorating Company, 1892–95}}

**Lilies (Corey Memorial Window)**, made by the Tiffany Glass and Decorating Company in 1892–95, shows what that achieved in architecture. It was one of several windows the firm designed for Christ Reformed Episcopal Church in Chicago, dedicated to Francis Edwin Corey and his wife, Vernera Leonard Corey. When the church was demolished in 1962, the windows were moved to a church in Tinley Park, Illinois. Lilies, a frequent Tiffany subject, were a natural choice for a memorial because of their association with resurrection. Look at how the glass itself does the modelling: the veining of petals and the gradations of green in the leaves are carried by colour and texture within the glass.

In the 1890s Tiffany also began to use Favrile glass for lamps, the objects for which his studios are best known today. The collection's **Lamp with Hanging Head Dragonfly Shade and Mosaic and Turtleback Base** (by 1906) shows the form at its most inventive, with dragonflies circling the lower edge of the shade.

{{artwork:185905|inset}}

The design is attributed to **Clara Driscoll**, and her story is an important correction to the idea of Tiffany as a lone genius. Tiffany approved patterns as artistic director but made relatively few lamps himself. Driscoll headed the Women's Glass Cutting Department and designed most of the firm's lamps. The dragonfly, with its delicate wings and changing colours, suited the medium perfectly.

## Honest construction

In Britain, the Arts and Crafts movement, inspired by thinkers such as John Ruskin and William Morris, argued for honest materials, visible craftsmanship and design rooted in function. Its influence shaped several works in the collection.

Edward William Godwin's ebonized **Sideboard** (designed 1867, made about 1876) is an early landmark of this change. Against the heavy Gothic Revival furniture of the time, Godwin offered a spare, linear composition of verticals, horizontals and open space, influenced by Japanese design. Its elegance won him clients such as James McNeill Whistler and Oscar Wilde.

Mackay Hugh Baillie Scott's **Manxman Piano** (1897), made by John Broadwood and Sons, solves a practical problem with wit. The upright piano was an awkward piece of furniture, so Scott enclosed it in an oak cabinet. The opened doors and lid serve as sounding boards, and inside are handcrafted metal fittings, including candleholders.

> The measure of these objects is not only how they look, but how well they do their work and how honestly they show how they were made.

## Toward modern design

By the turn of the century, the ideals of the movement were turning into something new. In Glasgow, Charles Rennie Mackintosh designed a ten-legged white **Table** (1902) for an exhibition of the new style in Moscow in 1903; its stark whiteness is said to have startled even his progressive Viennese counterparts. In Vienna, Koloman Moser designed a **Cruet Stand** (1904/05) for the Wiener Werkstätte, a geometric silver lattice that rejects historical ornament altogether. In the Chicago area, Prairie School architect George Washington Maher, working with Louis J. Millet, designed a **Fireplace Surround** (1901) for the Patrick J. King House, part of his approach of repeating a signature motif throughout a building to unify inside and out.

## What to look for

When you explore the decorative arts in the collection, try these questions:

- **What is it for?** Notice how the form follows the function, or sometimes playfully disguises it.
- **What is it made of?** Favrile glass, ebonized wood, perforated silver and glazed earthenware each have their own qualities.
- **Who made it?** Behind many famous names stood teams of designers and craftspeople, like Clara Driscoll.
- **Where would it live?** Most of these objects were designed as part of a room. Imagine the interior they were meant to complete.

These are useful things, made with an insistence that usefulness and beauty belong together.
