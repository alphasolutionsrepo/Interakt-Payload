We tend to meet ancient Egypt through its dead. Mummies, coffins and tomb paintings dominate our picture of the civilization, and it is easy to conclude that Egyptians were preoccupied with death. The objects in the collection suggest almost the opposite. Nearly everything made for an Egyptian tomb was made in the service of life: food that would never run out, servants who would never tire, a body that would never decay, a family that would stay together. The tomb was a toolkit for continuing to exist.

This essay walks through that toolkit, from the grand to the thumbnail-sized, using works in the collection that span more than two thousand years.

## A body that lasts

The core idea is simple. For a person to survive death, the body had to survive too, or at least something that could stand in for it. Mummification did the practical work, and its most tangible traces in the collection are the four **canopic jars of Amenhotep**, made during the reign of Amenhotep II (about 1427–1400 BCE). Amenhotep was an overseer of building works at the great temple of Amun at Karnak, and his jars came from a tomb at Dra Abu el-Naga on the west bank at Thebes.

{{artwork:120264|inset}}

Each jar once held one of the organs removed during embalming: the liver, lungs, intestines or stomach. Now empty, they are still eloquent. Look at the stoppers: each is a hand-modelled head, perhaps Amenhotep himself, perhaps one of the four sons of Horus, the deities charged with guarding the organs. The hieroglyphic inscriptions on the bodies of the jars call on a different goddess to protect each set of contents. Protection here is layered: a sealed vessel, a divine guardian, and written words that keep working as long as they exist.

The same logic scales up to the **Coffin of Nespahertahat**, an inner coffin from Dynasty 21 (about 1069–945 BCE). In that period elite Thebans were often buried not in lavishly decorated tomb chapels but in nested sets of wooden coffins, and the painting moved onto the coffins themselves. Nespahertahat's lid shows him with arms crossed on his chest, and its surfaces are crowded with gods and goddesses. The coffin is a portable tomb, carrying its own images and texts wherever the body went.

A millennium later, the idea was still alive. The gilded cartonnage **Funerary Mask** from the first century BCE, probably from Hawara, gave the deceased an idealized, imperishable face. Strung around the neck are small heart amulets. For Egyptians, the heart, not the brain, was the seat of thought, feeling and memory, and it had to be kept safe.

## Small objects, big protections

Amulets are among the most numerous objects in any Egyptian collection, and among the easiest to overlook. They were worn by the living and wrapped into the bandages of the dead, and each form carried a specific power. In the collection you can find:

- a **heart amulet** in glass (Dynasty 18), shaped like a small jar with handles, protecting the heart for the judgement of the soul;
- a **djed pillar** in faience, the stylized backbone of Osiris and a sign of stability;
- a **tyet**, or "Isis knot", often placed at the neck of the mummy;
- amulets of **Taweret**, the hippopotamus goddess of childbirth, and **Bes**, the lion-maned protector of women and children.

The blue-green faience of many of these pieces was not merely decorative. The colour evoked vegetation, the Nile's flood and renewal. A mummy sheathed in such colour was surrounded by images of regeneration.

{{artwork:128013|wide|A net of faience beads, laid over a mummified body, with the scarab of the rising sun at its centre}}

The **Bead Net Funerary Shroud** of Dynasty 26 (664–525 BCE) makes that symbolism explicit. Worked into the net of faience beads is Khepri, the sun god in his morning form as a scarab beetle. Just as the sun was reborn every dawn, the deceased hoped to rise into a new existence. Draped over the body, the shroud wrapped the dead in a promise of sunrise.

## Help for the hereafter

The Egyptian afterlife was not a place of idleness. It resembled an idealized Egypt, with fields to plough and canals to clear, and someone had to do that work. Hence the **ushabti** (or shabti), a figurine inscribed with a spell instructing it to answer on the owner's behalf whenever labour was demanded. The collection includes a tamarisk-wood shabti of Nebseni from early Dynasty 18 and a faience ushabti of Horudja from Dynasty 30, more than a thousand years apart and still doing the same job. Some burials contained hundreds of them, one for each day of the year plus overseers to keep them in order.

{{artwork:120268|inset}}

Other tomb furnishings provided transport and supplies. A painted wooden **Model of a River Boat** from Meir (about 1875 BCE), complete with oars, mast and a crew, ensured that the deceased could travel the Nile of the afterlife just as the living travelled the real river.

## Remembered by name

The last ingredient was memory. Egyptians believed that speaking a person's name, and reciting the offering formula, sustained them. Stelae were placed where visitors could see them and read the texts aloud. The vividly painted **Stela of Amenemhat and Hemet** (early Dynasty 12) shows the couple in eternal youth before a table heaped with food; every recitation of its words renewed that meal. Much earlier, the limestone **Stela of Tjenti and Nefret** (about 2540 BCE) gathers a family, down to a small granddaughter with a finger to her lips, into a permanent family portrait.

> The Egyptian tomb was not a monument to loss. It was a machine for keeping someone present.

The **Stela Depicting the Funeral of Ramose**, from the reign of Ramesses II, shows the ritual that activated all of this: the Opening of the Mouth. Priests burn incense, pour libations and read from a papyrus while a jackal-headed figure supports the coffin. The ceremony restored the senses to the dead so they could eat, drink and speak again.

## What to look for

When you browse the Egyptian works in the collection, try asking of each object: what problem was this solving? A jar protects the organs; an amulet guards against a particular danger; a figurine supplies labour; a stela keeps a name in circulation. Seen this way, the objects stop being grave goods and become something more human: careful, practical and hopeful plans for a future that their makers intended to last forever.
