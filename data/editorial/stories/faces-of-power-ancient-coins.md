Coins are the most widely distributed artworks of the ancient world. A sculpture stood in one place; a coin passed through thousands of hands, crossed seas and outlived the city that made it. The collection holds several dozen Greek and Roman coins, and together they tell a compact history of how images were used to project identity, confidence and power. These notes are a guide to what to look for.

## A city in your palm

Greek coinage began in the Aegean world in the late seventh and sixth centuries BCE, and each city-state struck its own. Since many of the people who handled coins could not read, the image itself had to identify the issuer. Cities chose emblems as recognizable as a modern logo.

The island of Aegina, a great naval power, put a **sea turtle** on its silver staters, like the example in the collection from 510–485 BCE. The reverse shows not a picture but the deep, windmill-like square left by the punch that drove the metal into the die. After Aegina lost its maritime dominance to Athens in the mid-fifth century, its coins switched to a **land tortoise**, as seen on a later stater of 404–350 BCE. It is a striking case of a civic symbol quietly adjusting to political reality.

Other cities chose emblems that advertised their wealth or their myths:

- **Metapontum**, in southern Italy, grew rich on farming and stamped an **ear of barley** on its coins, later pairing it with the grain-crowned goddess Kore.
- **Thebes** used the distinctive oval shield of its army.
- **Knossos** on Crete struck a square **labyrinth**, instantly recalling the Minotaur and the story of Theseus and Ariadne.
- **Taras** (modern Taranto) showed its legendary founder riding a dolphin, the animal said to have carried him ashore.
- **Corinth** chose **Pegasus**, the winged horse, paired with Athena, who in local myth tamed him with the first bridle.

## Athena's owl and the value of sameness

The most famous civic badge of all is the helmeted profile of Athena on the silver **tetradrachms of Athens**. The collection's example belongs to a type struck over a long span, from the early fifth century until the late fourth. While other cities updated their designs, Athens deliberately kept its archaic-looking goddess, with her almond eye and fixed smile, and the owl on the reverse. The conservatism was the point. The coins' reliable weight and silver purity made them trusted across the Mediterranean, and a familiar design reassured anyone accepting them that they were getting the real thing.

## Masterpieces in miniature

Some Greek mints treated the coin as a canvas for serious art. Syracuse, in Sicily, is the classic example. Its tetradrachm of 474–450 BCE shows the nymph **Arethusa** ringed by leaping dolphins. In myth she fled beneath the sea to Sicily and emerged as the freshwater spring that still flows in Syracuse. On the reverse, a charioteer drives his team while a winged Nike flies overhead to crown the horses, a reference to victories in the chariot races. A later Syracusan tetradrachm, of 413–399 BCE, shows Arethusa again with a four-horse chariot, and another, of 310–307 BCE, shows Persephone crowned with grain, since Sicily was believed to be the place where Hades carried her off.

Look closely at the engraving on these coins. Die cutters worked in reverse, carving into metal at a tiny scale, and yet they managed individual locks of hair, the flutter of drapery and the tension in a horse's legs. A tetradrachm from Myrina in Asia Minor, showing the god Apollo crowned with laurel (189 BCE or later), is often cited as one of the most beautiful coins of antiquity.

## When the ruler became the image

Early Roman coins, like Greek ones, carried gods and personifications. That changed in 44 BCE, when Julius Caesar became the first living Roman to have his portrait placed on coinage. The collection's **denarius portraying Caesar**, issued in 42 BCE, two years after his assassination, shows him laurel-crowned; the reverse gathers symbols of power and prosperity, including a globe, a rudder and a cornucopia.

{{artwork:5588|inset}}

From then on, the ruler's face became the standard front of a Roman coin, and the back was used to broadcast achievements or intentions. The effect was a kind of mass media. The collection lets you follow it through several reigns:

- a silver tetradrachm of about 37–33 BCE, issued by Mark Antony, pairs his portrait with that of **Cleopatra VII**, whose image takes the front, the more prestigious side;
- a gold aureus of **Otho**, who ruled for only a few months in 69 CE, with his double chin and carefully curled hair;
- gold aurei of **Hadrian** and **Antoninus Pius**, products of a prosperous and confident age;
- an aureus of **Caracalla** in breastplate and military cloak, with the god Serapis on the reverse;
- a **solidus of Constantine I**, struck in 324–325 soon after he became sole emperor, which deliberately echoes the clean-shaven, youthful look of Augustus and Trajan to link him with celebrated predecessors.

> On a Roman coin, a portrait was never just a likeness. It was a statement about legitimacy, carried in every purse.

The story continues into the Byzantine world with a rare gold **solidus of Empress Irene** (797–802), one of the very few women to rule the empire in her own name. She wears a pearl-hung crown and holds an orb and a cross-topped sceptre: regalia traditionally associated with male emperors, claimed here without apology.

## Tips for looking

When you browse the coins in the collection, zoom in and check both sides. Ask what the image identifies (a city, a god, a ruler), what the reverse is promising, and whether the metal is silver, gold or electrum, a natural alloy of the two. Small as they are, these objects were among the most persuasive images their societies ever produced.
