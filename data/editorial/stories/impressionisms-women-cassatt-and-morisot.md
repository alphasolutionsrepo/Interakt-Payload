The popular story of Impressionism is often told through a handful of men: Monet by the river, Renoir at the dance hall, Degas backstage at the Opéra. Yet two of the movement's most consistent and inventive participants were women. Berthe Morisot exhibited in the very first Impressionist exhibition in 1874 and in all but one of the eight shows the group held. Mary Cassatt, an American, joined them at Degas's invitation and exhibited in four. Neither was a guest or a curiosity. Both helped define what Impressionism was.

The collection holds five paintings by the two artists. Seen together, they show how two very different painters worked within the social limits placed on women in nineteenth-century Paris, and how those limits shaped a distinctive, and distinctly modern, body of work.

## Where a woman could paint

For a respectable middle-class woman in 1870s Paris, much of the city that fascinated the Impressionists was off-limits or awkward to visit alone. Cafés at night, backstage corridors, racecourses and dance halls were not places Morisot or Cassatt could easily set up an easel. Formal art training was restricted too: women were not admitted to the École des Beaux-Arts until the late 1890s, and drawing from the nude model was largely closed to them.

Art historians have long noted how this shaped their subjects. Their paintings are full of drawing rooms, balconies, gardens and dressing rooms, spaces where women lived and where they could work unobserved. It would be a mistake, though, to see this as a narrowing. In their hands the private sphere became a laboratory for the same questions of light, surface and modern life that preoccupied their colleagues.

## Berthe Morisot: the feathery touch

Morisot was the more radical painter in terms of technique. She came from a cultured Parisian family, exhibited at the official Salon in the 1860s, and was closely connected to Édouard Manet, whose brother Eugène she married in 1874.

{{artwork:11723|inset}}

*Woman at Her Toilette* (1875–80) shows her at her most daring. A woman seen from behind adjusts her hair before a mirror, surrounded by a haze of lavender, pink, white, blue and grey. The brushstrokes are loose and quick, almost like handwriting, and in places the canvas seems barely covered. The subject of a woman dressing was common among male painters of the period, often with an erotic charge. Morisot's version is intimate but not voyeuristic: the woman is absorbed in her own routine, not presented for a viewer. Look for the signature, which Morisot placed along the bottom edge of the mirror, as if her name, like the reflection, might dissolve at any moment.

In *Woman in a Garden* (1882–83), she applies the same slashing strokes of acid green and blue-orange to the figure as to the foliage around her. Person and setting are painted with a single vocabulary, so that the woman seems to be made of the same dappled sunlight as the garden. It is a thoroughly Impressionist idea, pushed further than many of her male contemporaries dared.

## Mary Cassatt: structure and intimacy

Cassatt arrived at Impressionism by a longer road. Born near Pittsburgh in 1844, she trained at the Pennsylvania Academy of the Fine Arts before moving to Europe, where she studied the old masters and settled in Paris. Early on she travelled widely to learn from them. *After the Bullfight* (1873), painted during a stay in Seville, shows a torero in full costume. It reflects her interest in Spanish painting and in the path of Manet, who had also looked hard at Velázquez.

By the late 1870s she had found her own direction. *On a Balcony* (1878–79), shown at the 1880 Impressionist exhibition, presents a woman in a garden surrounded by flowers in bursts of red, blue, yellow and pink. The telling detail is her reading matter: a newspaper, not a novel. Even at home, Cassatt's women are connected to the world beyond the garden wall.

{{artwork:26650|inset}}

Her masterpiece in the collection is *The Child's Bath* (1893). A woman sits with a small child on her lap, washing the child's feet in a basin. We look down on the scene from above, and the figures fill almost the entire canvas. The composition is built from strong outlines, bold patterns, like the striped dress, and flattened space, all lessons Cassatt drew from Japanese woodblock prints, which she studied closely after a major exhibition of them in Paris in 1890.

{{artwork:111442|wide|The Child's Bath, 1893}}

> Cassatt's mothers and children are tender without being sentimental. They are absorbed in tasks, not posing for us, and that absorption is what makes them feel true.

Notice where the two heads meet, both looking down at the same point in the basin. The whole picture turns on that shared gaze.

## Two painters, one movement

It is tempting to treat Morisot and Cassatt as a pair simply because they were women, but their work is very different. Morisot dissolves form in light and movement; Cassatt builds it with line and structure. Morisot's surfaces feel spontaneous and airy; Cassatt's, especially in the 1890s, are firm and deliberate.

What unites them is a shared insistence on painting modern life from the inside. They show women as readers, as mothers, as people absorbed in their own tasks and thoughts. Set their work next to other scenes of women in the collection by their male colleagues, such as Degas's *The Millinery Shop* or Renoir's *Two Sisters (On the Terrace)*, and the difference in viewpoint becomes clear. Morisot and Cassatt were not looking at women; they were looking alongside them.

When you look at their paintings, try asking a few questions:

- **Who is the painting for?** Is the figure performing for a viewer, or absorbed in her own world?
- **Where are we standing?** Cassatt, in particular, often places us very close, as if we were part of the household.
- **How is the paint applied?** Compare Morisot's flickering strokes with Cassatt's clear contours.

Their careers were long and influential. Cassatt also played an important role in encouraging American collectors to buy Impressionist painting. Both deserve to be seen not as the women of Impressionism, but simply as two of its finest painters.
